🌩️ The Tesla Coil — voltage by resonance

In 1891 Tesla built a transformer that works like pushing a child on a swing. A small primary circuit (a capacitor and a few turns of wire) rings at one frequency. A tall secondary coil with hundreds of turns has its own natural frequency, f = 1 / (2π√(LC)). If the two match, every push lands at the right moment, energy builds up in the secondary, and a few kilovolts grow into hundreds of thousands of volts, enough to throw sparks through the air. Tune the primary capacitor until the lightning roars.

How it works

Two tuned circuits

A spark gap repeatedly dumps the capacitor into the primary, which rings at f₁ = 1/(2π√(L₁C₁)). The secondary coil and its top-load ring at their own f₂. Energy only moves across well when f₁ ≈ f₂.

Resonance, not turns ratio

A normal transformer multiplies voltage by its turns ratio. The Tesla coil adds a resonant gain on top: energy builds up over many cycles, so the output can be far higher than the turns ratio alone would give.

Q sets the sharpness

The secondary's quality factor Q decides how tall and narrow the resonance peak is. A higher Q gives a bigger voltage boost, but you have to tune it more precisely.

From sparks to radio

Tuned LC circuits that respond to only one frequency are exactly what a radio needs to pick one station. Tesla's coil work fed straight into radio and remote control and wireless power.
