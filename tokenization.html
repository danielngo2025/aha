✂️ Tokenization — how an LLM reads text

A language model doesn't see letters or words — it sees tokens: chunks of text, each mapped to a number. Common words are usually a single token; rarer or longer words get chopped into pieces; and — surprisingly — the space belongs to the token (" the" is different from "the"). This is why models "think" in ~4-character pieces, why they miscount letters, and why some languages cost more to process. Type below and watch your text get sliced.

Where do the chunks come from?

Nobody picks tokens by hand. Start with every letter as its own piece, find the pair of pieces that sits side by side most often, and glue it into one new token. Repeat. This is byte-pair encoding (BPE) — type your own text to try it.

Why tokens matter

Not words, not letters

The vocabulary (~100k tokens for GPT-4) is learned so common text is compact. "the" is one token; "twinkling" might be "tw"+"ink"+"ling". The model only ever sees these pieces, never the raw characters inside them.

Spaces travel with words

Most tokens start with their leading space, so "cat" mid-sentence (" cat") and "cat" at the start are different tokens. It's a quirk of byte-pair encoding that trips up people expecting clean word splits.

Why models miscount letters

Ask an LLM how many "r"s are in "strawberry" and it may fumble — it sees a couple of tokens, not the individual letters. The information is literally hidden inside the token.

Tokens = cost & limits

Context windows and API bills are measured in tokens. Rare words, code, and many non-English scripts use more tokens per character — so they're slower and pricier to process.

The dominant scheme is byte-pair encoding (BPE): start from single bytes and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, until you have a fixed-size vocabulary. This demo uses a small hand-built vocabulary with greedy longest-match — the real thing has a far larger, learned vocabulary, but the behaviour you see here is the same.
