📻 Radio Remote Control — Tesla's boat (1898)

At Madison Square Garden in 1898, Tesla steered a small boat around an indoor pool with no wires, while the crowd shouted commands and the boat obeyed. Some people thought a trained monkey was hidden inside. The real secret was tuning: the boat's receiver was a resonant circuit that only responded to its frequency, so it could ignore other transmitters. He called it a "teleautomaton". Today the same idea flies drones and opens garage doors. Tune your transmitter, then drive.

What made it work

Pulses become commands

Each radio pulse tripped a coherer in the boat. Its clockwork logic stepped through rudder and motor states, so a series of pulses meant left, right, go or stop.

Tuning is the key

An untuned detector reacts to any burst: rival stations, sparks, even lightning. A resonant LC circuit, the same idea as the Tesla coil, passes only a narrow band of frequencies. Untick "tuned" with the rival on and see what happens.

The radio patent fight

Marconi usually gets credit for radio. In 1943 the US Supreme Court struck down key claims in Marconi's tuning patent, partly because of earlier work by Tesla and others.

Everywhere today

Drones, RC cars, key fobs, garage openers and Wi-Fi channels all rely on the same idea: many transmitters share the air, and each receiver is tuned to listen to only one.
