💧 Niagara Falls — AC proves itself (1895)

The War of the Currents was settled by a waterfall. In 1895 Westinghouse switched on the Adams power station at Niagara Falls, built on Tesla's AC patents. Water dropping about 41 m spun turbines driving giant 5,000-horsepower generators, and in 1896 the power went 42 km to Buffalo at a stepped-up 11,000 volts. No DC system could reach that far. The power from falling water is P = η·ρ·g·Q·h: efficiency × water density × gravity × flow × height. Bring generators online and pick a transmission voltage to see if Buffalo lights up.

Why Niagara mattered

The bet of the century

The Niagara commission weighed DC, compressed air and even rope drives. It picked Westinghouse's AC, built on Tesla's patents, which was a huge vote of confidence in an unproven system.

Each generator ≈ 3.7 MW

Each one was rated 5,000 hp (≈ 3.7 MW), far bigger than anything before. Three ran at first, and by 1904 there were ten. They made two-phase AC at 25 Hz, which fed straight into Tesla's induction motors.

Step up, ship, step down

Transformers raised the generator voltage to 11 kV for the trip to Buffalo, which cut the current and so the I²R loss. Transformers at the other end brought it back down for streetcars and factories.

The template for every grid

Large central plants far from cities, high-voltage lines, and local step-down: the grid you plug into today still follows the Niagara design.
