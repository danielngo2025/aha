🔋 Wireless Power — from Wardenclyffe to your phone

Around 1901 Tesla began building Wardenclyffe Tower on Long Island, a 57 m tower meant to send messages, and eventually power, across the world without wires. It never worked. The money ran out, and the physics was against him: a coil's magnetic field fades roughly with the cube of distance, so the coupling k between two coils collapses once the gap is bigger than the coils. A century later the same idea came back at the scale where it does work: the charging pad under your phone. Pick a scenario, then slide the coils apart.

Why it failed then and works now

Near field only

A coil's magnetic field falls off about as 1/d³ once you're farther away than the coil is wide. Inside that zone, almost all the energy can transfer. Outside it, almost none does.

Resonance stretches the reach

If both coils are tuned to the same frequency (the Tesla coil trick), energy builds up over many cycles, so even weak coupling can carry a useful share. Efficiency depends on k·Q, not just k.

What Tesla actually tried

Wardenclyffe wasn't meant to work like two coils. Tesla hoped to use the Earth and upper atmosphere as conductors. After J.P. Morgan withdrew his funding, the tower was abandoned and finally torn down in 1917.

The idea lives on

Qi phone chargers, electric toothbrushes, RFID cards, implanted medical devices and EV charging pads all use resonant inductive coupling over centimetres, where the physics works.
